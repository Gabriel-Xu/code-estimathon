Plasmid DNA constructs often consist of a promoter sequence, followed a by a desired gene, and ended in a transcription termination or polyadenylation signal sequence.

Plasmid-mediated resistance is the transfer of antibiotic resistance genes which are carried on plasmids.

Plasmid pHT01 is generally stable in both B. subtilis and Escherichia coli, and can be used for protein expression in these host strains.

Plasmid RNAIII is a non-coding RNA found in bacterial plasmids including pIP501.

Plasmids are considered replicons, units of DNA capable of replicating autonomously within a suitable host.

Plasmids are introduced into bacteria for such purposes as transformation, cloning, or transposon mutagenesis.

Plasmids are transmitted from one bacterium to another (even of another species) mostly through conjugation.

Plasmids become unstable after a certain amount of DNA has been inserted into them, because their increased size is more conducive to recombination.

Plasmids may be either high copy number plasmids or low copy number plasmids; the regulation mechanisms between these two type are often significantly different.

Plasmids may carry genes that provide resistance to naturally occurring antibiotics in a competitive environmental niche, or the proteins produced may act as toxins under similar circumstances, or allow the organism to utilize particular organic compounds that would be advantageous when nutrients are scarce.

Plasmids must regulate their copy number (average number of plasmid copies per cell) to ensure that they do not excessively burden the host or become lost during cell division.

Plasmids often carry multiple antibiotic resistance genes, contributing to the spread of multidrug-resistance (MDR).

Plasmid vectors are circular strands of DNA found in virions that are used in genetic engineering to integrate new genes into a host cell genome.
