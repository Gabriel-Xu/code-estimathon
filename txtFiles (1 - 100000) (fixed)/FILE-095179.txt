Terry Wayne Ward's funeral service turned out to be the perfect sendoff for a guy who "couldn't give a damn about most material things," according to his now famous obituary.

The atypical service was held Saturday at Geisen Funeral Home in Crown Point, where his daughter works and where she said her final goodbye to her one-of-a-kind father.

Jean Lahm, of Lowell, did so with smiles, laughter and funny stories about her dad, who died Jan. 23. Ward was 71. His attitude, however, could be that of a 17-year-old, or a 37-year-old, or possibly a 7-year-old, depending on his mood.

Not only did Ward's obit reflect his life. His life reflected his obit, which can't be said for most obits I've read.

Most obits read like fill-in-the-blank form letters, written with all the character of a closed casket. It's as if they're compiled by TV police detective Joe Friday from "Dragnet" – "All we want are the facts, ma'am."

Not Ward's obit, though, which went viral on social media and has since been shared by countless news outlets across the country."

He retired from AT&T after 39 years of begrudging service, where he accumulated roughly 3,000 rolls of black electrical tape during the course of his career (which he used for everything from open wounds to "Don't use this button" covers)," Lahm wrote in her father's obit.

Ward's obit didn't only describe him, it defined him, in a wonderful and memorable way. Thanks entirely to Lahm, who didn't tell a soul she was planning on writing a humorous obit for her always-humorous father."

When I showed my family, they absolutely loved it. Right from the bat, they said it was perfect," she told me.

Just as her obit reflected her father's life, so did his funeral service. With lots of laughs about his storied life. For instance, the story about Ward when he was employed by AT&T, working out of the Lake Village substation in Newton County."

It was pretty cool because I would be driving in that area and I would see him from time to time," Lahm recalled to guests Saturday. "One day I was driving down the road and sure enough I saw his AT&T truck pulled off to the side.""

He was working on a large box there, and he had it opened up with what looked like hundreds of different-colored wires pulled out all over the place. I remember thinking how complex. I was pretty impressed. I pulled up next to him and rolled down the window."

How's it going?" Lahm asked her father."

Oh, pretty good!" Ward replied. "It's just times like these that I wish I wasn't colorblind."

This was one of several personal stories Lahm shared with mourners. It reflected the lighthearted spirit of the obituary she wrote from her heart, not from her head."

I love that humor resonates with people in regards to an obituary," she told me after the funeral. "Being in the funeral industry, I've seen that when families can come together, celebrate a life and truly laugh, it's what helps them heal."

Because of that obit, who knows how many people now know about Ward. Most of them agreed they wished they had met him when he was alive."

What really mattered to me most was that we were able to celebrate his life with a proper sendoff," Lahm said. "It was with all the people who knew and loved him, laughing and laughing."

Many of those people gathered again at the funeral home's reception center after the funeral service."

You better believe a few of us tilted back a Heineken in his memory," Lahm said.

Ward's family read many online comments from strangers who expressed an interest in attending the funeral for a man they never met. Yet, through his obit, they somehow did.
