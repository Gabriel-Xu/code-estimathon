Ambulances will be given longer to reach all but the most serious calls, NHS bosses have today revealed. Pilot schemes being run in parts of the country will give emergency call handlers an extra two minutes to decide whether to despatch an ambulance. Current rules mean call handlers have just 60 seconds to gather the information they need. The short time limit has prompted concerns that ambulances are being sent to patients who do not need them. Health secretary Jeremy Hunt told MPs the changes will 'improve patient safety'. Emergency call handlers will be given two minutes extra to decide whether to send an ambulance to patients, in all but the most serious, life-threatening calls, NHS England have said . In a written statement to Parliament, he said giving staff 'very limited extra assessment time would ensure that ambulances are better deployed to where they are most needed and would allow a faster response time for those patients who really need it'. 'There is significant evidence to suggest that giving call handlers extra assessment time to make the right decision for the patient could improve clinical outcomes and improve their chances of survival,' he said. It comes after the health secretary asked NHS England to consider new measures to help address 'an unprecedented increase in demand on ambulance services' in the last two months. The new arrangements will be put in place in pilot areas in the areas covered by the London Ambulance Service and the South West Ambulance Service. The plans will give call handlers up to 120 seconds longer to get all the information they need from a person making a non-life-threatening 999 call before deciding on the right response. Mr Hunt said the new plans will not include those calls which are immediately life-threatening - categorised as Red 1 calls and requiring a response time within eight minutes. The first pilot is expected to start in February, NHS England said. Professor Keith Willett, NHS England's national director for acute care, said: 'We are piloting these new arrangements because on medical grounds, we believe they will increase the availability of ambulance vehicles and paramedic staff, providing patients with a better service and improving their chances of survival, especially those with the most serious conditions.' In the pilot sites, a small number of potentially life threatening conditions, such as overdoses and certain types of gunshot wounds, will also be upgraded from the Red 2 category into the higher-priority Red 1 category so they receive a faster response than is currently the case. Professor Willett said: 'It's not about relaxing standards. 'By acting with slightly less haste on the calls, we believe we can get to more patients with more speed. 'Some conditions will be upgraded to Red 1 status, requiring a more urgent response than is currently the case.' NHS England said evidence shows that less than 10 per cent of 999 calls are for genuinely life-threatening conditions, yet currently around 40 per cent are classified as such, partly due to the lack of time the call handler has before the clock starts for determining whether an ambulance has arrived within the target time. To meet the current target of reaching 75 per cent of patients categorised as Red 2 within eight minutes, ambulance trusts frequently send vehicles to 999 callers before they have determined the exact nature of the problem. They may also send more than one vehicle, such as an ambulance and a rapid responder. On average, more than 20 per cent of vehicles are cancelled before reaching the scene, wasting resources which could be used for other patients. Mr Hunt said: 'At present, ambulance services are allowed only 60 seconds before the clock starts to decide what the right course of action is for that individual patient. Note: This story originally appeared in the Journal Sunday magazine on March 3, 1988.RUSSELL and Elaine Yates had been married nearly 16 years before they decided to have children.It was Christmas Eve, 1980, when he brought it up. They were getting older. If they were going to think about having a family they should start, he said. He thought he was ready for the responsibilities.The Yateses had weathered some adversity in their years together. They were so young when they got married. They were only 18 - just six months removed from Pilgrim High School, in Warwick, where they had been sweethearts.He had survived a hitch in Vietnam. Elaine's brother had not. Russell's tour of duty ended when he escorted the body home.Elaine put up with Russell's carefree ways. He was young and restless. He liked fast cars - he always seemed to have a different Corvette - and he liked to have a good time. Sometimes she ran with him; other times she didn't stand in the way."I was foolish as a husband," Russell says now. "I expected her to accept an awful lot."But they had a lot of good times, too. And they were still very much in love. They managed to build a comfortable life together, he mostly with his brawn, she with her brains. Now, with their mid-30s approaching, they decided it was time to start a family.ON OCT. 15, 1981, Kimberly Ann Yates was born. On her second birthday, after a family party at which the age of the next-youngest relative was 13, Russell and Elaine decided Kimberly Ann should not have to grow up alone. A year later a second daughter, Kelly Ann, was born.But children did not bring husband and wife closer together. Elaine thought it was time for Russell to settle down; Russell, hot-headed and stubborn as always, was reluctant to change his lifestyle."That's when we started to draw our lines on what we'd tolerate in our marriage," Russell Yates says. "I wouldn't let her threaten me by holding the kids over my head."During an argument in August 1985, Elaine Yates threatened that she would get even, that Russell would be sorry he didn't listen. She said she would move out - in the middle of the night - and take their two children with her."I blew it off as just someone blowing off steam," recalls Yates, now 40.Three weeks later he came home from work, after 2 a.m., to discover that his wife was not in their bed, and his children's beds were also empty. The day was Aug. 27, 1985.TWO AND A HALF years have elapsed since that night. Russell Yates has not laid eyes on his family since. He has no idea where they are, and has never heard whether they are all right.No one else has admitted seeing his wife and children, either.A missing-children agency based in Newport and, at various times, six private-detective agencies hired by Yates have investigated the disappearance. The case was also investigated by the Warwick police for two years, as well as by the state police and the attorney general's office.Yet no one has been able to determine the whereabouts of Yates's family.What Yates says he has found is indifference among law-enforcement officials and a state law that does not consider his children missing.They have not been abducted, he has often been told; they are with their mother.THEIR EXTRAORDINARY separation is a long way from how it all started for Russell M. Yates Jr. and Elaine C. Pigeon.They met in freshman English class at Veterans High School, back in 1961. At 14, they were in love. And for four years - first at Vets, later at Pilgrim, where they were transferred after it opened, in 1963 - they went steady."They were the cutest kids," Russell's mother, Vivian Yates, recalls.No one was surprised when they said they wanted to get married. They were best friends, as well as lovers.Russell's mother thought they were too young. But, she says proudly, they had already saved enough money to buy a house and furnishings.On Jan. 22, 1966, they became husband and wife.AFTER GRADUATION they got jobs at the Leesona Corp., in Warwick, where both their fathers worked.Marriage was no big deal for Russell; Elaine gave him the freedom he wanted. "I wanted the rewards of married life, but I still wanted the fun of being a bachelor," Russell now says.That first year of marriage, their lives took an unexpected turn. In October, Russell received a letter from the U.S. Selective Service. A few months later, he was bound for Vietnam.In Vietnam, Yates was stationed near his brother-in-law, Joseph Pigeon, a sergeant in the 101st Airborne, the Screaming Eagles. They managed to get together on occasion, keeping each other's spirits up with talk of home.Then one day in August 1968, shortly before both their tours of duty were over, Yates got word that Pigeon had been killed in action.A few days later, Yates received a letter from his brother-in-law, written hours before his death. He wanted Russell to buy him a rope hammock; enclosed was $10. His platoon was sleeping in the mud, and Joseph wanted the hammock to hang between two trees. Yates still has the letter.WHEN YATES GOT BACK to Warwick, he resumed his carefree ways."When I came home I didn't want any restraints - I wanted some freedom," Yates says. "I didn't want to be told what to do, and what I shouldn't do."After he had been home for about a year, he left Leesona to sell real estate. Then he started a construction business.During this time, Elaine Yates worked at Old Stone Bank, and then, in 1977, switched to Fleet National Bank. As part of her work, she taught herself how to use computers, which had become an essential tool in the banking industry.As a couple, Elaine and Russell Yates were still very much in love."Every time you saw them they were holding hands," Mrs. Yates says.In 1981, after leaving the construction business and spending some time bullraking on Narragansett Bay, Yates bought Copperfield's, then a seedy men's pub in rented space above an auto-body shop on Warwick Avenue. About a year later, a fire in the auto-body shop burned Yates out.It took him nine months to buy the building and remodel it, installing four pool tables, numerous sports video games, and a split-level horseshoe-shaped bar. It is decorated with trophies, earned by the sports teams Yates sponsors, and, with both cable television and a satellite dish, there always seems to be a ball game on the dual big screens above the bar."It seemed like I was living here," Yates says. "But it didn't bother Elaine. She understood. When we were remodeling she'd come down and clean up. She did the books."IT WAS SHORTLY after Yates had purchased Copperfield's that Kimberly Ann was born. Kelly Ann followed three years later - and so did the problems.The situation came to a head on the first weekend in August 1985. Russell stayed out all night Friday; Saturday morning Elaine found him aboard his boat, a 32-foot cruiser docked at Masthead Marina, in Warwick. He was with another woman. It was, Russell says now, not the first time he had been unfaithful, and his wife knew it.She yelled. Russell tried to calm her. She left in a rage, running up the dock to her car, where Kimberly and Kelly were waiting. Elaine went to her mother's summer house, on Point Judith, where she and Russell had planned to spend the day at the beach with the children.About an hour later Russell joined his family, and for the rest of the afternoon not a word was spoken about the incident. Because the afternoon seemed to be going smoothly, Yates recalls, he thought Elaine had forgiven him. She had forgiven his indiscretions before.But as they were packing the car to leave, she pulled free when he tried to hug her. Russell was stung. Through all their problems, he still loved her, and thought she still loved him.BACK AT HER MOTHER'S cottage, they left the kids in the car and went inside to talk. The morning's quarrel flared again, more passionately than before. Russell stormed out of the house and sped off. About a mile down the road he pulled over, and started to cry. He couldn't leave things that way. He turned his car around and went back.Russell says he wanted to talk. But not all the rage had drained from Elaine. It only intensified when he said he wanted to take Kimberly back to Warwick. Elaine said no.Russell says that when he started to get the child's clothes, Elaine tried to kick him in the face. Then she slapped him "two or three times." Pulling away, he hit his head on the door. He lost his temper and swung back, striking her in the forehead. It was a punch he would long regret.The diamond ring he was wearing cut Elaine's forehead. Russell rushed her to South County Hospital, where the staff members saw a woman 5 feet tall and weighing 107 pounds who had been struck by a barrel-chested 185-pound man with thick arms and strong hands.A social worker told Elaine about shelters for battered woman, giving her the names of five such places in Massachusetts. Elaine had indicated that Russell would find her if she stayed in Rhode Island.BUT THINGS SEEMED to get back to normal - at least for about three weeks. Then, on Monday, Aug. 26, Russell, as he usually did, came home from Copperfield's to have dinner with his family, and then helped put the children to bed. At about 8 o'clock his mother-in-law, Mrs. Pigeon, stopped by to visit. She stayed about 45 minutes and left.Sometime after 9, Elaine brought up the incident on the boat. She wanted to argue. He didn't. He left the house and went back to Copperfield's.It was a night like a lot of others at Copperfield's. The crowd stayed late, agonizing over a 10-inning Red Sox loss that dragged past midnight. It was the Red Sox' 15th loss in 18 games, and the regulars groused about what was wrong with the team.It was after 2 a.m. by the time Yates locked up and made the 10-minute ride to his house, a split-level Colonial on the waterfront in Warwick Neck that he had built with his own hands in 1978. He parked his Blazer in the driveway next to his wife's station wagon and went in the front door."I went downstairs, got a soda and made myself a sandwich. I put the TV on for a while," Yates says. "Normally my wife would hear me, come down and talk to me for a while, see how the night went."After a while, she still didn't come down."Russell finally called to Elaine. Getting no answer, he went to their second-floor bedroom. The room was empty.He went to check on Kimberly, now almost 4. He found a bed that had recently been slept in.In the other bedroom he found the crib of 10-month-old Kelly Ann, also empty."The baby bottle was still in the crib," Yates says.Yates called his mother-in-law. There was no answer. He then drove to Mrs. Pigeon's house, in Warwick. No one was home. The only sound was the bark of her Old English sheepdog, in the enclosed porch.Yates drove to Mrs. Pigeon's summer cottage, on Point Judith, arriving just before 7 a.m. No one was there. A neighbor toldYates that no one had been at the house that night.YATES DROVE back to his house and reported the disappearances to the Warwick police. He says he was told he had to wait 24 hours before he could file a missing-person report. The police, though, say they have no record of a call from Yates on Aug. 27. A call was logged in at 8:40 a.m. on Aug. 28.The police turned the investigation over to Detective Stanley Butterworth, whom Russell Yates told of his marital problems with Elaine. In his investigation, Butterworth concluded that Elaine Yateshad voluntarily left her home. Butterworth's findings were supported by a telephone call to the police from Mrs. Pigeon on Sept. 7."Mrs. Pigeon stated she received two phone calls from Elaine, and that she was okay and the kids were okay," said Capt. Roger Burlingame, of the Warwick Police's detective bureau. "We had no reason on earth to believe she was lying to us."At that point there was little the police could do. There was no foul play involved and, under Rhode Island law, no crime committed. Under state statute, the police could not consider the Yateschildren to be missing. They had not been abducted, according to the law. They were with their mother, who, as a parent, had legal right to them.The law says that a person can be charged with child snatching if he or she "intentionally removes . . . or detains" a child with intent to deny another person's right to custody "under an existing decree or order of a Rhode Island Family Court." The charge is a felony, punishable by up to two years in prison.
