The tax rant and voluminous disclosure gives Bush a chance to talk about his wealth (his campaign puts it between $19 million and 22 million) on his own terms, while delivering a dig at Clinton, who is still under fire for her exclusive use of a personal email account while serving as secretary of state.Unlike the most recent Republican nominee, Mitt Romney, whose refusal to release his personal financial disclosures metastasized into a campaign-crippling issue, Bush was downright eager on Tuesday to come completely clean, tweeting alerts and supporting graphics an hour ahead of the actual disclosure teasing his 33 years of returns as “more than any presidential candidate in history.”The staging and packaging of this document dump was a sleight of hand aiming to distract from the disclosures themselves — the fact that Bush is very much a one-percenter who has aggressively monetized his family connections to amass significant wealth amid a recession — and from several bad business dealings with questionable associates that have already been well-chronicled.The former Florida governor’s wealth dramatically increased after he left office. Bush, who often emphasizes his business experience and brags that he has “signed the front side of a paycheck,” was worth $1.3 million when he left the governor’s mansion in 2007 and started a consulting business, Jeb Bush and Associates, with his son, Jeb Jr.He also joined a number of boards, including Tenet Healthcare, Rayonier and Geo Fossil Fuels. He further supplemented his income (to the tune of $8.1 million) by giving paid speeches to audiences such as TD Ameritrade, The Fertilizer Institute and Poongsan Corp., a Korean company that manufactures coin blanks (discs onto which coins are struck) and other metal products.He also made a good sum as an adviser to Lehman Brothers, which paid him $1.3 million a year. After Lehman went bust in the financial crisis, Barclays, which took over much of the financial firm, paid Bush $2 million a year. (Bush mentions Barclays only in his written description of his tax returns.)While Bush presented his disclosure as historic in scope, only the last 13 years of returns, are actually new; he released 20 years of tax filings in 2002 while serving as Florida governor. But after Romney, being transparent is a low bar to clear — and, more importantly, Tuesday’s document flood was a way to sharpen a contrast with Clinton, the Democratic front-runner.“There’s always some secrecy associated with Clinton, so this is a good contrast for us,” said a Bush confidant familiar with the candidate’s thinking. “She’s transparency under duress; Gov. Bush is willful transparency.”On his website where the tax returns were posted, Bush slickly packaged the 33 years of tax data for mass consumption, offering a written overview in his own voice along with photographs, an accompanying letter from a former IRS general counsel who provided an independent review of the returns and “fast facts” summarizing the filings.Jumping out from the slickly packaged overview of his returns: a large, colorful graphic comparing Bush’s 36 percent number alongside Clinton’s 30 percent tax rate.“He is setting up a contrast on an issue that has the potential to be extremely damaging to Hillary Clinton’s campaign,” said Steve Schmidt, a GOP strategist who oversaw John McCain’s 2008 campaign. “It will be a reversal of 2012, and Democrats are going to have an extremely hard time when the disclosure requests are coming from the right.”Clinton’s campaign press secretary, Brian Fallon, said Tuesday that Clinton “fully expects to release her tax returns again this time, as she did the last time she ran for president.” He also pointed out that 30 years of Clinton’s tax returns, from 1977-2006, are already public.In the near term, the full release may enable Bush to get ahead of whatever stories about his finances are in the offing, and it sets a marker that may be hard for his GOP rivals to meet (Marco Rubio, for one, hasn’t even been paying taxes for 33 years).“I think other candidates you’d categorize as front-runners may have to look at this and react and decide when and how much they’re going to disclose,” said Rob Stutzman, a GOP strategist in California.Bush’s action Tuesday also serves as a signal to the GOP’s donor class that he has learned an important lesson from Romney’s refusal to release any tax returns (he eventually released two years of information), which helped Democrats add definition to their sketch of the nominee as a callous, greedy plutocrat.“We made it an issue last cycle that it should not have been,” said Saul Anuzis, a former candidate for Republican National Committee chairman from Michigan. “When Democrats want to play class warfare, hiding this kind of information plays into their hands. This is a smart way to handle it, being preemptive rather than reactive.”But some experts speculated that Bush could be overcompensating. While the top statutory rate for wage income is 39.6 percent, a 36 percent effective rate is unusually high for a wealthy individual with a wide range of investments. Much of his income should have been taxed at the much-lower capital gains rate; although Bush’s team notes that the bulk of his income comes from speeches, boards and other business dealings, not investments, and is classified as “ordinary” income, which may explain the higher rate.Tax historian Joe Thorndike, for one, suggested Bush’s seemingly high effective tax rate could be the result of “political calculation.”“I’m not sure why someone with his business interests doesn’t have more from capital gains,” Thorndike said.“I suspect there was some very shrewd political calculation going into this — he knew he was running for president and he knew he was planning to for some time,” he added.Also notable in Bush’s returns is his level of charitable giving, which significantly ramped up as his wealth increased and as he moved closer to his presidential bid.He reported giving just $16,000 to charity in 2008, and tax records show he gave less than 2 percent of his income to charity during his first seven years out of office (by comparison, President Barack Obama’s most recent return showed his donations amounting to almost 15 percent of his income).
