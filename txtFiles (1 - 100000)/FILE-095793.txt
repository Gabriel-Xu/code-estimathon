"The way Elaine stole her children, it is not a crime," says June Vlasaty, founder and head of the Society for Young Victims, a missing-children agency in Newport.Vlasaty contends that the law must be changed. If a court order has not been issued, she says, the law allows one parent to deny the other parent the right to see and care for their children.The law has been brought to the attention of two lawmakers, Rep. Norma B. Willis of Jamestown and Newport and Sen. Thomas A. Lynch of Warwick, who agree that it needs to be changed."It's incredible to me," says Willis. Elaine Yates "just took off and, because there was no specific custody order at that point, it falls through the cracks."Lynch has asked the Senate's legislative counsel to draw up a new law, one that would make it a crime for a parent to prevent the other parent from having access to their child. Lynch also wants the legislation to allow prosecution of someone who aids the abducting parent.The lawmaker says that legislation will "definitely" be introduced to the General Assembly this session, and that it has an "excellent" chance of passing.BUT REGARDLESS of what the existing law says, Vlasaty thinks that the police are not taking the Yates case seriously enough. Elaine Yates, Vlasaty says, should be charged with child abuse."People don't realize the psychological impact on the children," Vlasaty says. "All children live a lie when they are abducted. They're told the other parent is dead, or didn't want them anymore. Both are traumatic."Vlasaty, who formed her agency in 1975 after the abduction and murder of 5-year-old Jason Foreman, of Peace Dale, said abducted children are usually given a new name, are often kept out of school, and are afraid to play with other children, for fear of revealing their identity. Sometimes, Vlasaty says, the children's appearances are changed and, on occasion, they are passed off for the opposite sex."They're living a life on the run," she says. "They're living like criminals."NOT SATISFIED with the police's response, Russell Yates started his own investigation. For 2 1/2 years, his quest has been relentless. He has pressed both the Warwick and the state police to do more, and has urged the attorney general to lodge criminal charges against both his wife and his mother-in-law.He has fought legal battles in Family Court and Superior Court, hiring a battery of lawyers and detectives. He has enlisted the aid of missing-children networks, complained to the governor's office, and mailed more than 12,00 5letters, mostly to pediatricians and elementary schools throughout New England, asking if anyone has seen his children ("Somewhere out there," says Yates, "I have a daughter who should be in school. In order to get in school they need medical records.") He has spent more than $40,000 on his search.SIX WEEKS AFTER his family had disappeared, Russell Yates went to Family Court to gain legal custody of his children. Without a custody order, missing-children networks would not advertise his children as missing.A month later, after the request for custody had been published in newspaper legal notices and efforts by the court to contact his wife had failed, Yates won temporary custody.But the custody order was still not enough to get the police to issue a warrant for Elaine Yates's arrest. The obstacle was that there was no evidence to prove Elaine knew that Russell had court-ordered custody of their children. In Rhode Island, a person may use legal notices to divorce someone, but such notices are not sufficient to inform one parent of the other parent's legal custody of their children."The crime occurs when a person, knowing about the order, willfully disobeys the order, with the intent of depriving the other parent of custody," Special Asst. Atty. Gen. Barry Capalbo says. "The key is the person must know about the order."Capalbo says that the state law on child snatching may be applied to the Yates case, even though the court order was issued after Elaine Yates disappeared, if the authorities can determine that she knows of the order.FROM THE BEGINNING, Russell Yates has suspected that his mother-in-law had a hand in his wife's disappearance. So did the police. They questioned Mrs. Pigeon the day the disappearance was reported. Then they followed her. The day after Russell told Mrs. Pigeon that her daughter and grandchildren had vanished without a trace, she had her hair done, went to a bakery, and then visited a friend.'We're confident that the mother knew where she was," retired Warwick Police Chief John Coutcher says.Detective Butterworth, now also retired, says that during his investigation he learned that Elaine Yates was in Connecticut "for a while," and possibly Florida. Through Russell Yates, he learned of a family wedding in Michigan that Mrs. Pigeon was to attend, as well as, perhaps, her daughter. He says he had authorities in Michigan check out the reception, but Elaine Yates never showed up.Butterworth says he never was able to find Elaine Yates.In the weeks after his wife and children disappeared, Russell Yates was not satisfied with the answers he got from Butterworth. Yates says, for example, that the detective told him he had a telephone number for his wife, but would not give it to him, and so Yates had Butterworth subpoenaed to give a deposition in Family Court.Under oath, Butterworth acknowledged that "in a roundabout way" he had told Yates he knew how to contact his wife. But he said in the deposition that he had not contacted her. He said he did not know where she was, that he had no way of contacting her, and that he had not talked to anyone who had contact with her.Then why did he recommend closing the case?He said he had based his decision on information supplied by a "confidential informant."In a chamber conference with a judge a few days later, Butterworth said that his confidential informant was Mary Pigeon.TEN DAYS after Butterworth had made his statements, Mrs. Pigeon gave a deposition, on Dec. 19, 1985. In it she said that she had had only two brief telephone conversations with her daughter since she had disappeared, on Aug. 27. One call was on Aug. 29, the other in mid-October. Previously, she had told the police that the second call had come within days of the first.Mrs. Pigeon also said that she had received a call in November from an unnamed woman who said her daughter was well and living with a family. The woman provided no other details, and she did not ask the woman any questions, Mrs. Pigeon said.Asked whether she had told Butterworth that she had a telephone number where her daughter could be reached, Mrs. Pigeon said, "I never said that. No, I did not."In another deposition, given a year later as part of a $1 million civil suit filed against Mary Pigeon by Russell Yates, Mrs. Pigeon reverted to her original story, saying that both calls had come within 10 days of the disappearance. She also now denied receiving a call from a stranger.In both depositions, Mrs. Pigeon said that she had left the Yateses' house about 8:45 p.m. on Aug. 26, and driven to her Narragansett house with her Old English sheepdog. At about 10 p.m., she said, she was picked up by a couple and driven to a house in Charlestown, where she cared for an elderly woman until the couple returned at midnight.Mrs. Pigeon testified that she did not know the elderly woman's name, where in Charlestown she lived, or the names of the woman's daughter and son-in-law, who had picked her up and then driven her home.Mrs. Pigeon also says she left her summer house at 6:45 a.m., and drove home to Warwick. Russell Yates contends that she did not return home until about 2 p.m. that day.Neil Philbin, the lawyer who filed the civil suit against Mrs. Pigeon, says that the woman's explanation of her activities "is so proposterous and incredible that it is apparent . . . she is lying."The civil suit, filed Sept. 4, 1986, is Yates's attempt to get Mrs. Pigeon to disclose what she knows about her daughter's disappearance. The suit is expected to come to trial within the next few months.In her depositions, Mrs. Pigeon said that she knew of the incident on the boat and the argument at the beach house, because her daughter told her about them. The past 2 1/2 years, she says, "have been pure hell."Hardly a night goes by without Vivian Yates's waking up, her grandchildren in her thoughts. Some nights she finds herself talking to them. She wonders aloud whether she will ever see them again.She used to pester Chief Coutcher about the investigation every day until he retired, in December. He never once avoided her. She praises him for that.Mrs. Pigeon has never called the Warwick police to ask the status of the investigation."We figure she knows where they are," Capt. Burlingame says. "I'm sure she would be much more distraught if she hadn't heard from her daughter for two years."IN ANOTHER DEPOSITION, filed Dec. 19, 1985, as part of the Family Court proceedings, a co-worker and friend of Elaine Yates's said that Elaine had talked of the incidents on the boat and at the beach house. Christine Reiff, of Cranston, also said that Elaine had talked about leaving her husband for two months before her disappearance."I don't know when or if, but I know she had to get away," Reiff said. "She was afraid of Russ. She thought he might really hurt her."Reached by telephone recently, Reiff declined to discuss Elaine Yates and hung up.Russell Yates denies that he abused his wife - except for that one regretted punch. He contends that she used the one time that he hit her to call upon the resources available to battered women to help her disappear.Philbin, the lawyer who filed the civil suit against Mrs. Pigeon, adds that questions of domestic abuse should not cloud the case."She doesn't have the right to circumvent the judicial process by making a unilateral decision to take away the children from him," Philbin says of Elaine Yates. "He had just as much right to custody as she did."IT WAS AFTER he got court-ordered custody of his children that Yates enlisted the aid of the Society for Young Victims. The Society then tapped a nationwide network of agencies that scour volumes of computer data on missing children. The Society turned up a few bits of information.June Vlasaty, the Society's head, is certain that Elaine Yates lived in Massachusetts for a time, and believes that she has been to Florida, where she has a cousin in Fort Lauderdale, and possibly to Arizona.But Elaine Yates has left no paper trail - no bank accounts, credit-card applications, driver's licenses, medical or school records for the children . . . nothing."This woman is so well hidden, she has to know how to hide," Vlasaty says. "The only way she'll be found is if she slips up - says something to someone."In January 1987, Yates, unhappy with the lack of results of the Warwick police, complained to the state police. It was 17 months since his children had disappeared and their names were not even listed in the National Crime Information Center; only Elaine Yates's name was listed.Finally, prompted by the state police, the Warwick police in February 1987 entered the names of Kimberly Ann and Kelly Ann Yates into the computers.Burlingame says that Russell Yates's criticism of his department is unfounded. He says that his department has put hundreds of hours into the case.LAST SPRING, one of Yates's lawyers, Susan McGuirl, who was part of the prosecution team in the first Claus von Bulow murder trial, asked the attorney general to present a case against Elaine Yates to a grand jury. McGuirl says that enough evidence exists to charge Yates with child abduction and issue a warrant for her arrest.The evidence includes two affidavits - one signed by Vlasaty and the other signed by Joseph Garrow, a federal marshal from Detroit who volunteers to help locate missing children - stating that Mary Pigeon had admitted to them in late 1986 that she had told Elaine about the court order giving Russell custody of the children.Capalbo, the special assistant attorney general, has declined to comment on whether the case has been, or will be, presented to a grand jury. "I can't compromise the investigation," he says.Yates, Vlasaty and McGuirl met twice last summer with the state police, asking them to get involved in the investigation.Finally, on Aug. 21, 1987 - nearly two years after Elaine Yates and her two children had disappeared - the investigation was handed over to the state police."But nothing changed," State Police Capt. Michael Urso says. "There was nothing else we could do, because the mother, as far as we knew, had not been notified of the custody order."THE MANY PEOPLE looking for Elaine Yates often come back to the question of why she would run, not only from her husband, but from her friends, her past, even her name.Only Russell Yates thinks he knows the answer."She did this to get even with me," he says. "She told my sister that if we got separated, she wouldn't want me to bring her kids around another woman. I would never do that. But this is her way of assuring that."If she returned, everyone agrees, the question of custody could be resolved."Russell doesn't want to take the children out of Elaine's life," Vlasaty says. "He just wants to see his kids again. He'd even give up custody."Capalbo says that the attorney general's office wants only to see the husband and wife resolve their differences in Family Court - "I'm not interested in a conviction."THE YATES HOUSE on the waterfront of Warwick Neck is quiet and sullen. Elaine's station wagon still sits in the driveway, its finish weather-beaten and its tires nearly flat. After his wife and daughters disappeared, Russell moved out, locking away all the house's memories. He has rarely returned.But today Russell Yates approaches the house - with the trepidation of a stranger.Inside, the place is dark and eerie. The shades are drawn. It is cold - lacking both heat and the warmth of a family.The living room is like a motel suite, furnished, but void of character. There are few pictures, and no family portrait. The only signs of life are in Kimberly's and Kelly's rooms, which are still cluttered with toys, just as they were the night 2 1/2 years ago when the children were taken away. Yates, his voice choked, his eyes welling with tears, vows that nothing will be changed until his children return."This is their home.
