They examined people’s responses to a promise being broken, kept and exceeded. And they say this is due to people overvaluing kept promises, which suggests ‘a general tendency in social systems to discourage selfishness and reward cooperation,’ they write. In one of several experiments carried out by the researchers they paired up participants and tasked one with solving 40 puzzles. For each puzzle solved, they would receive a monetary reward. However the other in the pair, the ‘promise-maker’, was tasked to offer their help in solving ten of them. When the promise-maker exceeded their task by solving 15 puzzles, the promise-receivers did not value that much more than when they stuck to the original ten. However, when help was only offered on five puzzles, the promise-receiver viewed their partner much more negatively. A similar study, which gave participants better or worse seats to a concert, elicited similar responses. ‘Breaking one’s promise is costly, but exceeding it does not appear worth the effort,’ the researchers conclude.
