Nothing out of the ordinary there. But there's a quirk that makes this case rather unique. Julia only became Julia AFTER she had already moved over land. You heard that right. No water necessary.So is this a sign of the apocalypse or is there some science behind how this happened? Let me correct the "no water necessary" line. That's a big overstatement. We all know that tropical systems need warm water to serve as fuel. But let's analyze exactly what that means. It's not as if these storms have giant vacuum cleaners that suck of the ocean water directly. The warm ocean water helps translate to more moisture-laded air directly above the surface of the water. It is this moisture rich air that serves to fuel the heat engine of a tropical cyclone. So to break things down to their most basic level, it is not the ocean itself directly fueling the storm, but rather the effect of enhancing the moisture of the air to be ingested. That fact appears to have played a critical role in how Julia came to be.Interaction with land is traditionally the kiss of death for tropical cyclones. The absence of ocean heat content eventually weakens the cyclone. Terrain, particularly mountainous, will weaken things too. But Florida is essentially one big swamp without mountains. In fact, the surface environment 25 miles inland from the coast can, at times, be rather similar to the environment 25 miles offshore. Dew points across eastern Florida where well into the 70s, providing a still moisture-laded environment (not dissimilar to what the storm would have experienced had it stayed offshore).Remember, it's not the ocean itself *directly* fueling the cyclone. If we can establish an environment with sufficient moisture & heat content, direct placement over water becomes unnecessary. Make no mistake...the warm waters of the Atlantic still very much did their job here. The inland environment was the result of proximity to those waters. Further aiding development, low wind shear values and a lack of mid level dry air presented favorable conditions for development.By Tuesday morning, the National Hurricane Center had given the area of disturbed weather (still over the Atlantic) a 0% chance of development. By late Tuesday afternoon, there were clearly signs of tropical organization.The upgrade occurred later Tuesday night with the center of circulation just barely onshore. Remember...we're not talking about a Tropical Storm that developed over Huntsville, Alabama. This was pretty darn close to the coastline. The closer you are to the water, but closer your environment is going to be to that of the water.So there you have it. Julia becomes the first Tropical Storm to be named in Florida while over land. Is the world as we know it breaking down? Nope. But Julia's development is cool because it forces us to look more in depth at just how Mother Nature operates. The necessity of a storm needing to be located over water is an over-simplification of things in this case.
